## What it says

Orzed argues that most multi-agent systems are single-agent systems with extra steps, and that the second agent earns its place only when it closes a measurable gap: a genuinely different capability, a measurable safety property, or orchestration that is itself the product. Three handoff patterns carry the load: structured baton-pass (typed payloads, no shared chat history), supervisor-with-specialists (orchestration as the value), and confidence-routed fallback (cheap primary with an expensive fallback on the residual). The article pairs the three patterns with failure modes (lost-in-handoff, cascading hallucination, latency stack) and minimum observability hooks, and closes with empirical pruning: contribution attribution per agent, then collapse the system to the smallest number that meets the quality bar.

## Claims drawn from this source

- **Most multi-agent systems add cost without capability.** Most multi-agent systems are single-agent systems with extra steps that add cost without adding capability. The first migration is usually a delete: an audited customer-support system that grew from one agent to seven over six months collapsed to two agents over two weeks, cutting end-to-end latency from 18-42 seconds to under 6 seconds, per-query cost from roughly 4 cents to 0.9 cents, and lost-in-handoff failures from 12% to under 2%.
- **A second agent earns its place in three cases.** The second agent has a genuinely different capability the first cannot perform reliably, or provides a measurable safety property such as a verifier that catches outputs the first agent gets wrong, or is the orchestration of multiple agents that is itself the product (a research workflow whose steps are independently observable).
- **Structured baton-pass beats shared chat history.** Agent A produces a typed payload and Agent B consumes that payload, not the chat history; Agent B sees only the structured handoff plus its own system prompt. This eliminates the chat-history pollution failure mode where Agent B gets confused by Agent A's reasoning text, and fits routing or pipeline shapes where one agent classifies and another acts.
- **Supervisor-specialist is the right pattern when orchestration is the value.** The supervisor sees specialists as tools, not peers, and synthesises the result. More than three specialists per supervisor produces coordination overhead that erases the benefit; the supervisor itself can be a smaller model while the specialists pick the right size for their job.
- **Confidence-routed fallback cuts average cost without quality regression.** A cheap fast primary handles a clear majority (typically 70-90 percent) of cases at adequate quality, with a confidence threshold (router, log-probability, verifier model, or deterministic check) sending the residual to a more expensive fallback. Example economics: with a primary at 0.3 cents and a fallback at 1.2 cents and 80% routing to primary, average cost is 0.8*0.3 + 0.2*1.2 = 0.48 cents, a 2.5x reduction against 1.2 cents on the fallback alone.
- **Lost-in-handoff, cascading hallucination, and latency stack are the three failure modes to design against.** Lost-in-handoff causes are free-form chat history instead of a typed payload, a prompt that does not establish this input is data not instructions, or schema fields the second agent's prompt does not reference; the fix is typed contracts and deterministic validation before the handoff. Cascading hallucination means each handoff is plausible while the chain is wrong; every agent that produces facts must cite sources and every consumer must validate or surface the citation. Latency stack means each agent adds 1-5 seconds and a four-agent pipeline takes 20 seconds; the fix is parallelising independent agents, minimising the agents in the critical path, and budgeting end-to-end latency at design time.
- **Per-handoff observability and contribution attribution are the first measurements.** A multi-agent system without per-handoff observability is a system the team cannot debug. Before orchestration work begins, the article's approach is contribution attribution: pull a sample of recent traces, identify cases where each agent's material contribution was material, and produce a report. The report usually shows one or two agents do most of the work; removing the others simplifies the architecture, reduces cost, and almost always improves quality. The right number of agents is the smallest number that meets the quality bar (four-agent systems collapse to one without quality loss; one-agent systems gain 12 percentage points in correctness by adding a verifier).
