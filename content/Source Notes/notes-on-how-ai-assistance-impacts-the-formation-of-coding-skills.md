## What it says

Anthropic's randomized controlled trial asks whether AI assistance speeds up learning a new skill or merely shortcuts the learning stage. In a study of 52 mostly junior Python developers unfamiliar with the Trio library, AI use led to a statistically significant drop in mastery (17% lower quiz scores, 50% vs 67%, nearly two letter grades, Cohen's d=0.738) with no statistically significant speedup, and the size of the effect varied by how participants used AI. Six interaction patterns were identified: low-scoring patterns (AI delegation, progressive AI reliance, iterative AI debugging) scored under 40%, while high-scoring patterns (generation-then-comprehension, hybrid code-explanation, conceptual inquiry) scored at or above 65%.

## Claims drawn from this source

- **AI assistance significantly reduces mastery of a new skill.** On a quiz covering concepts participants had used just minutes before, the AI group averaged 50% versus 67% in the hand-coding group, an 17% gap or nearly two letter grades (Cohen's d=0.738, p=0.01).
- **AI use does not significantly speed up learning a new skill.** Participants in the AI group finished about two minutes faster on average, but the difference did not reach statistical significance; every AI-group participant completed the second task while four of 26 control participants did not finish it.
- **The largest skill gap is in debugging.** The gap between AI and control groups was largest on debugging questions, where the ability to identify and diagnose errors is crucial for detecting when AI-generated code is incorrect.
- **Low-scoring interaction patterns score under 40% and rely heavily on AI.** AI delegation (n=4), progressive AI reliance (n=4), and iterative AI debugging (n=4) all showed less independent thinking and more cognitive offloading, with average quiz scores under 40%.
- **High-scoring interaction patterns score at or above 65%.** Generation-then-comprehension (n=2), hybrid code-explanation (n=3), and conceptual inquiry (n=7) use AI for generation plus explanations or conceptual questions, and showed average quiz scores of 65% or higher; conceptual inquiry was second fastest overall.
- **Time spent composing AI queries explains the lack of productivity gain.** Several participants took up to 11 minutes, about 30% of the total time allotted, composing up to 15 queries; AI is expected to be more likely to significantly increase productivity on repetitive or familiar tasks.
- **Control participants encountered and resolved more errors, likely improving their debugging skills.** Participants in the No AI group encountered more syntax and Trio errors, and the hypothesis is that they improved their debugging skills through independently resolving these errors.
- **Productivity gains on familiar tasks coexist with stunted skill development on new ones.** A separate observational study found AI can speed up some tasks by 80%, but that work measured productivity on tasks where participants already had the relevant skills, while this trial measures learning of a new library; the two results are in tension and the relationship is unresolved.
